Short Essay

Are LLMs a Crutch?

A hammer helps a carpenter build faster, but if he never learns to measure, it only helps him build badly at speed. That is how I read the statement that LLMs like ChatGPT, Claude, and Copilot must be a tool, not a crutch. The question is whether I’m still doing the thinking. We have been warned over and over again, and I think it’s fair. LLMs are fast and confident, which makes them tempting. If I submit whatever they produce, I get a grade without the learning behind it, and I risk academic dishonesty. They can also be wrong while sounding right, and that’s actually quite common.

On the other hand, they can sharpen my learning. For me, AI tools shorten my workload. It’s the assistant I always wanted and a wonderful tool for learning new things. For example, I pair AI with W3Schools to understand coding languages more efficiently and take a little stress off my shoulders. The ability to prompt a quiz will forever be crucial to my routine. My rule is simple: when I’m stuck, not when I’m starting, I attempt the problem first, then ask for help. After each session I ask myself, am I more capable, or just more dependent?

LLMs are only as valuable as the way I choose to use them. If I rely on them blindly, they become a crutch that weakens my growth. If I engage them after doing my own thinking, they become a tool that strengthens my learning. The responsibility is mine: to measure before I build, to think before I ask, and to make sure AI enhances my capability rather than replaces it.

Chapter and Life Analysis

5 laws of Gold

Wealth is ultimately a product of human effort, so the energy I invest in my studies, my work, and my ventures is the foundation of my financial future. The Richest Man in Babylon offers a disciplined framework for preserving and multiplying that effort. By saving at least a tenth of my earnings (Law 1), I convert labour into capital. By directing that capital toward productive assets such as businesses and investments that generate returns (Law 2), I allow my money to create value independently of my own labour. Seeking counsel from experienced people (Law 3) and avoiding ventures I do not understand (Law 4) reduces the risk of costly mistakes, while rejecting get-rich-quick schemes (Law 5) keeps my focus on sustainable, long-term growth. Together, these principles give me a practical strategy for building wealth steadily, regardless of the career or enterprise I pursue.

7 cures for a lean purse

The seven cures for a lean purse in The Richest Man in Babylon offer a disciplined route from earning money to keeping it. The first cure, to pay myself first by saving a tenth of everything I earn, is the foundation, because it converts income into capital before any spending takes place. The second cure guards against lifestyle creep, the tendency for expenses to rise automatically as income does until every raise is absorbed by “necessary” spending. By holding my living costs to the remaining nine-tenths, even as my income grows, I keep the gap between what I earn and what I spend widening rather than closing. The third and fourth cures direct that saved capital into investments that multiply while protecting it from loss through caution and sound advice. The fifth, sixth, and seventh cures extend the plan by encouraging me to make my home an asset, secure a future income for later years, and keep increasing my own ability to earn. Wealth, according to these principles, is built through consistent habits rather than high income, and I do not need to wait until I feel I can afford it. From this point forward, I will apply the ten percent rule to every dollar I earn, setting it aside first, protecting it from lifestyle creep, and putting it to work so that my effort today becomes lasting financial security.
